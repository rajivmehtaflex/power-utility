# Finding longitude at sea

**STE-inspired simplified English — approximate, not validated for ASD-STE100 conformity**

For centuries, sailors could find their latitude at sea. They read their latitude from
the Sun and the stars. But they could not find their longitude at sea reliably.

Longitude needs the time difference between the ship and a reference meridian. One hour
of difference equals fifteen degrees.

In 1714, the British Parliament passed the Longitude Act. The Act offered rewards for a
practical method. The method had to find longitude within half a degree. The test
condition was the end of a voyage to the West Indies.

Astronomers expected the answer to come from lunar distances. This method reads the
position of the Moon against fixed stars. The Moon acts as a clock in the sky. The
method worked. But it needed hours of calculation. It also needed a clear sky. So it
was never convenient for every navigator.

John Harrison was a carpenter and a self-taught clockmaker. He built sea watches
instead. His fourth timekeeper was named H4. H4 sailed toward Jamaica in 1761. The
recorded error of the trial was small. It was small enough to meet the Act's condition.

Even so, the Board of Longitude did not pay the largest reward at once. Payments
continued in stages until 1773.

How closely H4 would have kept time over decades of ordinary service is still not
known. Modern tests of copies suggest a caution. The performance may vary with oil,
temperature, and care.

After Harrison, the direction was clear. Keep time at sea. Compare the time with the
reference meridian. Turn the difference into degrees. Unlike lunar distances, the watch
method does not depend on the weather. This advantage was probably decisive.
